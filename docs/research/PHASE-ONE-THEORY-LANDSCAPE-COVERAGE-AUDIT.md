# Phase One Theory Landscape Coverage Audit

## Executive summary

This Phase One scan suggests that the biggest remaining coverage risk is **not** missing another agent loop or persona layer. It is missing theory families that give you explicit handles on **state persistence, action-conditioned update, replay/consolidation, controllability, viability regulation, social latent modeling, and evidence governance** under auditable computation. Across the literature and open-source landscape, the strongest candidates for comparison are therefore clustered around six families: **stateful cognitive architectures**, **predictive/action models**, **memory and consolidation systems**, **social/companion memory architectures**, **viability/homeostasis theories**, and **evaluation/provenance methods**. By contrast, many popular “agent” projects are useful as baselines or tooling layers, but they are weak evidence for any bounded functional-subject proxy by themselves. citeturn30view1turn30view2turn33search4turn13search8turn37view3turn36search5

A second high-confidence conclusion is that **explicit, inspectable state machinery matters** for your audit. SOAR, ACT-R, Sigma, NARS, CLARION, and Hyperon all give more direct leverage over state representation and control than prompt-centric orchestration frameworks do, even though their empirical maturity and tractability differ widely. SOAR and ACT-R remain valuable because they make memory, decision cycles, and learning modules explicit; Sigma is relevant because it tries to unify multiple functions in one probabilistic graphical substrate; NARS is relevant because it is built around adaptation under insufficient knowledge and resources; Hyperon is relevant because it offers a live neural-symbolic substrate with MeTTa and Distributed Atomspace, which closes a real theory-landscape gap even though its public evidence remains substrate-level and early-stage. citeturn30view1turn30view2turn31view3turn32search11turn29view0turn29view1turn29view2turn29view3

A third conclusion is that **memory systems deserve heavier audit weight than most current multi-agent frameworks**. Complementary Learning Systems, hippocampal replay/consolidation, continual learning, Letta/MemGPT, and reflective memory systems all speak directly to your target proxies for replay, long-term adaptation, serialized-state continuity, and evidence-gated recall. However, the public evidence still mostly supports bounded claims like “improved retrieval, summarization, or long-context behavior,” not continuous self/state or same-agent continuity in the strong sense. That distinction is critical for your claim ceilings. citeturn13search8turn13search14turn15search0turn37view0turn37view2turn37view3turn16search3turn16search4

A fourth conclusion is that **viability and affect should be audited primarily as control/regulation theories, not as human-likeness theories**. Active inference, predictive processing, homeostasis, viability theory, and homeostatic RL give formal or semi-formal ways to describe preferred states, regulation under uncertainty, and action/perception coupling. They are directly relevant to Gate2 and Gate3, but none of them, by themselves, license claims about selfhood, consciousness, or “real emotion.” They are best treated as bounded mechanism families for controllability and regulation. citeturn8search12turn33search4turn12search5turn12search3turn9search7

A fifth conclusion is that **social/companion-related systems are a real audit family, but also a major claim-inflation zone**. Generative Agents, Virtual Human work, Theory-of-Mind modeling, companion memory architectures, affective computing, and appraisal models can help with long-horizon social memory, role-consistent behavior, and user-modeling proxies. Still, the evidence generally supports only bounded claims about believable behavior, social simulation, or improved personalization. It does not support relationship learning in the strong sense, and it certainly does not support real emotion or stable companion benefit. citeturn17search1turn22search1turn22search13turn38view7turn21search4turn23search4

The final conclusion is governance-related: **before EGO-mainline admission planning, the comparison set should explicitly include evidence-governance methods** rather than treating them as afterthoughts. Mechanistic interpretability, causal scrubbing, intervention/ablation, red teaming, contamination/leakage defense, reproducibility badging, provenance schemas, deterministic replay, and agent benchmarks for memory and autonomy are all directly relevant because they determine whether a future architecture can make bounded, defensible claims under your computed-evidence provenance standard. citeturn27search4turn27search12turn28search0turn28search2turn27search22turn36search0turn36search2turn36search3turn27search2turn16search3turn16search4turn16search5

Throughout the matrix below, the **status field is provisional for comparative-audit priority only**. It is **not** a recommendation to adopt any architecture into EGO-mainline, and it is **not** a winner selection.

## Missing or underexplored theory families

The mandatory families you listed are enough to prevent the biggest historical gaps, but three additional coverage gaps still stand out.

First, **Common Model of Cognition** deserves inclusion even if it is not a final candidate architecture. The current SOAR overview notes that SOAR, ACT-R, and Sigma share enough structure that they helped motivate a Common Model of Cognition abstraction. For your audit, that matters because it can function as a **comparison schema** rather than as a winner. It gives you a way to ask whether different candidates actually cover the same underlying slots for perception, working state, long-term memory, action selection, and learning. citeturn30view1

Second, **adjustable autonomy and human/agent delegation control** are underexplored relative to your Gate2 and controlled-initiative goals. The literature review on adjustable autonomy exists because deploying “autonomous” systems in dynamic environments immediately raises handoff, override, and authority-boundary questions. For an EGO-adjacent project that explicitly cares about controllability and self-boundary, that family belongs in the coverage set before any admission planning. citeturn12search11turn12search23

Third, **memory evaluation itself has become a separate research family**, and it should be treated that way. LoCoMo, LongMemEval, MemoryArena, and StateBench are not just benchmarks; together they encode a moving consensus that memory claims should be tied to retrieval accuracy, temporal reasoning, useful downstream action, and abstention/update behavior across sessions. That is closely aligned with your evidence-governance posture and helps prevent inflated continuity claims from memory demos alone. citeturn16search3turn16search4turn16search1turn16search5

For later comparative audit, the most justifiable additions beyond the mandatory set are therefore: **Common Model of Cognition as a comparison schema; adjustable-autonomy/control-boundary work for Gate2; and modern memory-evaluation suites as admission filters rather than after-the-fact metrics.** citeturn30view1turn12search11turn16search3turn16search4turn16search5

## Theories most relevant to EGO-mainline admission

Theories and systems most relevant to eventual EGO-mainline admission fall into five clusters.

The first cluster is **explicit stateful cognitive architecture**: SOAR, ACT-R, Sigma, NARS, and Hyperon. They differ sharply, but they all expose machinery that is closer to your target than prompt-only orchestration is. SOAR and ACT-R provide explicit decision/memory cycles; Sigma attempts a unified substrate; NARS targets adaptation under insufficient knowledge/resources; Hyperon provides a persistent graph substrate with executable rewrites and distributed memory. If you are auditing for bounded mechanism proxies rather than surface behavior, this cluster must stay central. citeturn30view1turn30view2turn31view3turn32search11turn29view0turn29view1turn29view2

The second cluster is **memory and consolidation**: Complementary Learning Systems, hippocampal replay, continual learning, Letta/MemGPT, and reflective memory systems. This cluster maps directly onto Gate1, same-agent bridge, long-term memory, and parts of post-bridge admission. It is also the cluster most likely to produce false confidence if used sloppily, because better retrieval and summarization can look like continuity even when the underlying system is just doing stateful prompt assembly. That means these candidates are both highly relevant and highly in need of strict claim ceilings. citeturn13search8turn13search14turn15search0turn37view0turn37view2turn37view3turn16search4

The third cluster is **viability and regulation**: active inference, predictive processing, viability theory, and homeostatic RL. This cluster is especially relevant to Gate2 and Gate3 because it gives formal language for preferred states, constraint maintenance, action/perception coupling, and regulation pressure. Importantly, it is a better fit for “functional affect” than straightforward emotion-label systems are, because it begins from control and survival-like regulation rather than anthropomorphic labeling. citeturn33search4turn9search12turn12search3turn9search7

The fourth cluster is **social latent and companion memory**: Theory-of-Mind modeling, Generative Agents, virtual-human architectures, and companion memory systems. This is the right place to investigate Gate4 and companion-layer mechanisms. But it is also where you need the strongest decoupling between “socially plausible output” and “relationship learning.” These candidates are relevant because they operationalize user models, social memory, and interaction persistence. They are not relevant because they prove anything about real social understanding. citeturn22search13turn17search1turn22search1turn38view7

The fifth cluster is **evidence governance** itself: provenance/replay, contamination defense, red teaming, causal evaluation, and agent-memory/agent-autonomy benchmarks. Given your computed-evidence provenance standard, this should be treated as part of the architecture landscape, not as a purely external QA function. A future candidate that cannot support traceability, replay, contamination-resistant evaluation, and ablation/intervention testing should be weakly favored regardless of how attractive its high-level theory appears. citeturn36search0turn36search5turn28search0turn28search2turn27search12turn16search4turn16search5

## Theories that are interesting but should be deferred

Several families are important to keep in the matrix but should be **deferred** as direct admission candidates because they are either too abstract, too rhetorically overloaded, or too far from executable comparison.

The clearest examples are **enactivism, embodied cognition, autopoiesis, artificial life, and open-ended evolution**. These families are intellectually relevant because they remind you that long-run adaptation, coupling, self-maintenance, and bounded viability may matter. But they do not yet offer a comparably sharp engineering path for your Phase One question, which is coverage before admission planning, not a philosophy of life or mind. Their best role right now is to prevent overfitting to disembodied chat architectures and to sharpen evaluation questions. citeturn11search1turn8search23turn11search10turn26search14turn25search14

A second deferred group is **Global Workspace Theory and Society-of-Mind-style broad integrative theories**. These are useful abstractions and can influence architecture design, but they are too easy to over-read. In practice, they work better as comparison lenses than as near-term admission candidates unless they are instantiated in a more specific executable framework such as LIDA or a concrete broadcast/arbitration mechanism. citeturn32search2turn32search4turn7search0turn30view7

A third deferred group is **headline agent frameworks that primarily orchestrate LLM workflows**, especially BabyAGI, AutoGPT, and low-friction role-play frameworks. These projects are useful baselines for what prompt-and-tool orchestration can already produce, and they are relevant when you need to rule out a hardcoding/prompt-performance illusion. They are much less useful as positive mechanism candidates for bounded functional-subject proxies. citeturn38view3turn38view0turn38view1turn37view5

## Theories that risk claim inflation

The single biggest claim-inflation zone is anything that is **aligned in public discourse with consciousness, selfhood, or human-like inner life**. That includes Global Workspace Theory, active inference/FEP rhetoric, autopoiesis, and some companion/virtual-human narratives. These families can be useful mechanistically, but they also attract language that exceeds the actual evidence base, especially when moving from formal/regulatory accounts to claims about experience or subjectivity. citeturn32search4turn33search4turn11search10turn22search1

A second inflation zone is **affective computing and appraisal models**. These systems often do something real and useful at the engineering level: classify interaction states, modulate priorities, model appraisal, or adjust behavior. The inflation risk appears when these variables are treated as evidence for emotion in the strong sense instead of as design-time or control-time proxies. For your audit, these should be framed strictly as bounded functional-affect candidates. citeturn21search4turn23search4turn23search20

A third inflation zone is **Generative Agents, companion memory systems, and ToM-style social architectures**. Publicly, these are often discussed as if believable social continuity were evidence of relationship learning or stable social representation. The stronger reading is not warranted. The literature supports claims about social simulation, partner modeling, or personalization; it does not establish durable interpersonal learning in the sense your framing explicitly excludes. citeturn17search1turn38view7turn22search13

A fourth inflation zone is **popular agent frameworks**. AutoGPT, CrewAI, LangGraph, AutoGen, and BabyAGI can all make systems look more “agentic” by adding tools, memory wrappers, planning loops, or multi-step execution. None of that, by itself, is evidence for continuous self/state, agency, or long-term learning in the strong sense. For your project, they are more useful as falsification baselines than as positive evidence. citeturn38view0turn38view1turn37view4turn37view5turn38view3

Hyperon deserves mention here too. Its public materials use ambitious AGI-oriented language, but the repo and release materials still describe a pre-alpha/alpha-stage system with initial/basic components. That does not make it unimportant. It does mean any adoption rhetoric would outrun the public evidence substantially. citeturn29view1turn29view3

## Hyperon-specific assessment

Hyperon is best understood today as **a neural-symbolic substrate plus architecture family**, not as a validated full theory of mind. In the published project materials, **Atomspace** is a weighted labeled metagraph used to represent many knowledge types; **MeTTa** is the language that queries and rewrites those graph structures; and **Distributed Atomspace** is the scaling layer that lets the knowledge base span larger infrastructures and multiple cognitive components. The official overview emphasizes that MeTTa programs live as subgraphs and operate by querying/rewriting Atomspaces, while the alpha announcement says the stabilized core consists primarily of the MeTTa interpreter plus DAS in initial/basic form. citeturn29view0turn29view1turn29view2turn29view3

For your project, the parts of Hyperon that map most naturally are its **persistent inspectable state**, **graph-structured multi-memory representation**, **query/rewrite execution**, and **modular cross-process integration**. That gives it plausible relevance to Gate0 update, Gate1 replay, Gate2 boundary/control logic, candidate generation, long-term memory, and same-agent bridge experiments. The ecosystem around CogPrime/cognitive synergy also makes it one of the few live frameworks explicitly thinking about interoperability across multiple memory/process types rather than only one monolithic latent representation. citeturn29view0turn29view2turn30view7

That is exactly why Hyperon should be **included in the coverage matrix**. It closes a real gap. Missing it would leave out one of the most explicit current attempts at a graph-based, neural-symbolic, multi-memory, AGI-motivated substrate. However, inclusion in the coverage matrix should **not** be confused with a recommendation for EGO-mainline adoption. The strongest reasons **not** to adopt it directly at this stage are straightforward: the public codebase is early; the public release still frames core pieces as foundational/basic; the system’s architectural surface area is large; and the public evidence is much stronger on substrate construction than on tightly bounded demonstrations of the specific Gate0–Gate4 proxies you care about. citeturn29view1turn29view3

In short, Hyperon looks more like a **candidate substrate** than a validated mechanism package. It may eventually be useful as a platform for explicit state, symbolic memory transforms, and inspectable candidate generation, but the present public record does not justify stronger claims than that. It should therefore be treated as a **comparative-audit candidate with unusually high upside and unusually high integration risk**. citeturn29view0turn29view1turn29view2turn29view3

The bounded experiment that would be needed before any serious “use” discussion is also fairly clear at a high level: Hyperon would need to be tested **not** on broad AGI rhetoric or open-ended demos, but on a narrow, falsifiable provenance-locked benchmark that asks whether a serialized state, a fixed observation/action stream, and a fixed set of constraints produce auditable and replayable updates, replay/consolidation traces, controllability boundaries, and candidate-generation outputs under your computed-evidence provenance contract. Until it passes that kind of constrained audit against simpler baselines, the strongest defensible claim remains substrate-level only. citeturn29view1turn29view3turn36search5turn36search3

## What this research cannot prove

This audit cannot prove that any candidate architecture is “the one,” and it cannot prove EGO readiness, bridge readiness, companion readiness, mechanism validity in the strong sense, selfhood, consciousness, subjectivity, real emotion, real relationship learning, or stable user benefit. Many of the surveyed items are theoretical, prototype-level, or scoped to narrow domains, and even the stronger engineering frameworks usually validate workflow competence, game performance, social simulation, or memory retrieval under particular benchmark regimes rather than durable functional-subject mechanisms. citeturn29view3turn17search1turn37view0turn37view3turn16search3turn16search4turn27search2

It also cannot prove that “memory” means continuity, that “agent loops” mean agency, that “emotion models” mean emotion, or that “believable social behavior” means relationship learning. The current literature is strong enough to justify bounded claims about explicit state machinery, replay, regulation, planning, personalization, tool use, and benchmarked task competence. It is not strong enough to collapse those into stronger ontological claims. citeturn14search0turn37view3turn23search4turn17search1turn38view7

Finally, this audit cannot prove mechanism validity just because an open-source project exists. Hyperon, BabyAGI, OpenHands, CrewAI, LangGraph, Letta, and others are all useful parts of the coverage landscape for different reasons, but open-source availability only proves that an implementation exists and can be inspected. It does not prove that the underlying theory is correct, that the architecture generalizes, or that any observed success is free of benchmarking artifacts, prompt illusions, or contamination. That is exactly why the evidence-governance families belong in the same matrix as the theory families. citeturn29view1turn38view3turn37view6turn38view1turn37view4turn37view0turn28search2turn36search5
